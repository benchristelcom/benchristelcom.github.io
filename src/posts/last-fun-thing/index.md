# Last Fun Thing

_Should we go gentle into that good night?_

When I was a very small child, about three or four years old, my evening ritual
was called "Last Fun Thing." It took place between about 7 and 8 PM, after which
the bedtime story began. During this hour, my parents would play along with
whatever I wanted to do. Sometimes I would pick out a favorite toy, or put on
a puppet show, or watch a video (on tape!). Those were happy times — as far as I
can recall. I think I was usually satisfied with _one_ Last Fun Thing, but
perhaps my parents remember better. Did I storm and squall at the stroke of
eight? Did I protest? Did I plead my case for a _second_ Last Fun Thing? I'm
sure I must have. I was human, and I know what humans are.

It seems to be universal, this ritual of pre-bedtime entertainment. Universal,
and timeless. By 2020, the world was greatly changed,
I was of course grown up, and my bedtime was a whole two hours later than it
had been in those early days — but the Last Fun Thing lived on, in deed if not
in name. Covid had struck. I was working from home, and the lack of a commute
meant that I had more free time than I could remember ever having. It was
precious to me, abundant though it was, that wide-open expanse between 7:30 and
10, when I was fed and washed and free from obligations. I budgeted that time
— planned it out, scheduled it to the minute. I spent a lot of it watching
YouTube: mainly let's-plays, vlogs, and programming videos. The goal was to
have as much fun as I could in two and a half hours. 

You see, I was under a certain very common misapprehension: that happiness is
proportional to the average rate of fun.

```
H ∝ F/t
```

That is to say: the more time you spend having fun, say, each day, the happier
you will be — and the more "efficiently" you spend that time — the more fun
you can fit in per hour, and the greater the intensity of the enjoyment — the
happier you will be. This is a very simple, attractive, and *plausible* model.
It feels intuitively like the way happiness *should* work. The only problem with
this model is that every part of it is completely wrong.

No matter how many YouTube videos I watched, it was never enough. I always
wanted to squeeze in one more. Sometimes one more turned into two more, or an
hour-long binge. I would stay up late, get over-tired and be unable to sleep.
No part of this process really felt like "happiness", but I assumed that was
because I wasn't trying hard enough.

Very often, I went to bed with a feeling of regret. I *didn't have enough fun*.
There was *something else* that I should have done that would have been *more
fun*, if only I had thought of it in time. _I should have worked on that
project_. _I should have researched that question._ _I should have played
Minecraft._

I thought that maybe the problem was inefficiency — not enough fun per minute.
So I cranked up the playback speed on YouTube to 1.5x, then 2x. Still not fun
enough. So I took to watching two videos at once.

There was one time when I found myself simultaneously listening to an Alan Watts
lecture on Asian religions, and watching an unnarrated stream of a WWII flight
simulator. Someone was flying a fighter plane towards a dramatic sunset. On a
whim, I fullscreened the second video. And suddenly, I was in the head of that
fighter pilot. Completely focused, utterly alone. Nothing but air for miles
around me in all directions. Nothing but the sun and the roar of the engine in
my face.

In that moment, a strange calm came over me. I found I had no fear of death,
yet I knew I would fight hard to survive. I had no hatred for my enemy, yet I
knew I would bring all my skill to bear to shoot him down. My body had become a
machine — a machine that I was merely _watching_, as if it were part of the
aircraft.

And at the same time, I heard Alan Watts say, in his wry, unhurried baritone:

> It seems to me that the most interesting question in all of philosophy is:
> _What am I?_

_What am I?_ Am I this body? Am I the plane? Am I my enemy? Am I the sun and
the wind? Or am I beyond all of them? If I'm going to make it out of this alive,
what should I do now? And if I'm not going to make it out of this alive, what
should I do now?

And I began to understand.

I began to understand that time is not a vessel to be filled. It is only the
page number of a story we are being told. Time advances, moment by moment, at an
unvarying rate, and our reactions to what it brings define the quality of
our lives. Whether life is long or short, fast or slow, makes no difference.

The guns blazed, and pulled smoke from hot metal.

That night, I was pensive. The litany of regrets was there as always. But there
was also a weariness that I had not felt before. _If this is how I go to bed
every night_, I thought, _how much worse will I feel at the end of my life,
when the night of death is closing in?_ But I didn't know what to do, or what
to change.

In time, reviewing the day's regrets before bed became its own kind of ritual.
And, thanks to the random variance of life, some nights brought fewer regrets
than others. I started to notice patterns.

One of the most surprising patterns was that doing chores seemed to make me
happy. Some weekends, I found myself with a long list of to-dos. In spite of that,
I found I was rested at the end of those weekends. The weekend felt long and full,
like a satisfying meal. I was eager to get back to my day job on Monday. This
did not fit in with my theory of happiness — good old `H = F/t`. So I started
to investigate the psychological texture of those chores a bit more.

I found that, although I never _wanted_ to do chores before I started them, once
I had started it was easy to keep going. The work wasn't really unpleasant, and
I found that I didn't have any strong desire for it to be over. It wasn't fun,
but it was _[just okay]_.

[just okay]: https://defaanged.mataroa.blog/blog/life-without-a-computer-is-just-okay/

I also noticed that the time of day I was happiest was early in the morning:
while making breakfast, eating breakfast, and after breakfast but before work.
I did the same "ritual" every morning.

Based on these experiences, I came up with a new model of happiness.

```
H = 1/D
```

Happiness is inversely proportional to desire.

However, I couldn't bring myself to fully believe that this was true.

How can I take what I'm currently doing (watching a lot of YouTube) and turn it
into a _ritual_ that I do _without desire_ so that I will be happy? YouTube
seems optimized against this. It wants you to want more videos and to always
be looking for the next thing to watch.

The answer was Culture Machine. But that is a topic for another time.
