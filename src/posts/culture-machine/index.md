# What is Culture Machine?

I want to write a series of posts on Culture Machine, my YouTube-based TV and
radio simulator. I guess this is the first one!

What is Culture Machine? You can try it out right now at the link below.

- [Culture Machine](https://tv.benchristel.com/)

Your first reaction is likely to be "what" or "huh" or "it doesn't do much,
does it?" or "why would anyone want this?" Indeed, this is a large part of what
makes me want to write about Culture Machine. Everyone I've shown it to is a bit
baffled by it, and I think that says something about both it and them.

Culture Machine is what you get if you take all the dark patterns that Silicon
Valley tech corps use to addict and trick people, and flip them all on their
head. It's a vision of what technology *could* feel like, and what culture
*could* feel like, if it were made by different people with different
motivations. Our current culture doesn't have anything like it, and most people,
I think, have no frame of reference for it.

In spite of its strangeness, Culture Machine is my oldest hobby project that's still
"alive", and I use it almost every day, so there must be something special
about it. In this post I want to explore what that something might be.

## Sitting by the stream

Culture Machine lets you "tune in" to a virtual TV station made of YouTube
videos. The video that plays is whatever the station's broadcasting at that
moment; in all probability you will arrive in the middle of one. You
can't skip ahead. You can't pause. You can't go back. You *can* change the
channel, but: there are only four channels, three of them are audio-only, and
one of those just plays an audio loop of a clock ticking forever.

_Why would anyone want this?_ Well, the reason *I* wanted it, back in 2022 when
I started making it, was this:

> There are a lot of videos on YouTube. The bad ones look like clickbait. The
> good ones also look like clickbait. They're all competing for your attention,
> and as a result, just choosing something to watch is exhausting.
>
> If you just let YouTube autoplay it will feed you absolute garbage.
>
> The solution? Curate a set of videos that have some chance of being delightful
> or informative, and that have rewatch potential, and then just shuffle them.
>
> [Culture Machine README](https://github.com/benchristel/tv#culture-machine)

Okay, but... a clock ticking forever? Really? How is that "delightful or
informative"? In truth, I don't have much of an excuse for the clock channel,
aside from the fact that I basically never watch it.

Still, "delightful or informative" is at best a very imprecise description of
the videos Culture Machine contains. Ever since I started collecting YouTube
links, I've struggled to express what it is that makes me go "yes, that one."
Here is a brief catalog of some of my acquisitions to date — see if you can
spot the pattern:

- Live Turkish folk music
- The Lord of the Rings DVD extras
- Gilbert and Sullivan operettas
- Eddie Izzard's standup comedy
- Richard Feynman's physics lectures
- Stewart Brand's "How Buildings Learn"
- A four-hour Vedantic meditation retreat
- Adam Savage making movie prop replicas (and other things)
- Foraging and crafting videos by experimental archaeologist Sally Pointer
- A documentary about a Cornish fishing town
- A documentary about building a medieval castle
- Footage from Welsh TV archives
- The historical linguistics of English and its dialects
- Norwegian weaving in the 1930s (?)
- Gardening how-tos in Dutch
- Alan Watts lectures
- Classical music, jazz, blues, vocal, etc. concerts
- Celtic mythology lectures
- Jeeves and Wooster - the TV series
- Primitive technology
- Model railroads
- Forensic ecology

Have you guessed it yet? You never will. Not in a million years. You know how
I know? The information you'd need isn't in that list. It's not going to show
up in any summary or transcript.

The answer certainly isn't as simple as "I collect videos that I like." Once,
as I was musing at Channel 1, my sister asked me, "do you *like* Irish
farming content?" The short answer is "not really," but the answer I gave her
was "that's the wrong question."

A pervasive, almost axiomatic assumption of our worldview is that people should seek out things they
like, try to get them, enjoy them, and become happier as a result. This
view is wrong. Happiness doesn't work that way. You can't become happy by
chasing what you like. The way to happiness is to settle down into the good,
rich, wholesome muck of the world, and — to borrow a phrase from Zhuangzi —
wag your tail in the mud.

## A world without big tech

It's ironic, of course, that in order for these videos to exist and be available
to us, youtube has to exist, and youtube is close to epitomizing Silicon Valley
tech culture. In truth, I have very mixed feelings about YouTube because it's
really two things: a global database of videos that anyone can upload to
(good!) glued to a user interface that optimizes for addiction and conspiracy
theories (bad!) If nothing else, Culture Machine shows us a way to take the
good without the bad.

That's not to say it's without precedent, of course. I'll talk about the
precedents, maybe later, maybe in a future post. The topic I want to focus on
in this post is: *what the heck is it?*
